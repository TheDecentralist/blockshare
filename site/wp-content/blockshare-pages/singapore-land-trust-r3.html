Where Does Land
Serve the People

A Housing Policy Comparison of: Singapore, Copenhagen, Vancouver, and the Permanent Land Trust model. Singapore made housing worthless over time — and everyone's better off. Here's what that means for Vancouver, and why a Permanent Land Trust might be the most realistic path to the same outcome.

Based on: Elle Griffin, The Elysian, March 2026 · Analysis by: Ward Stirrat / Block Share
🇸🇬 Singapore State leasehold model
🇩🇰 Copenhagen Co-operative middle path
🍁 Vancouver Speculative private market
🌐 Permanent Land Trust Community-governed trust

In 1965, Singapore was a slum-riddled city-state with no natural resources, mass unemployment, and a housing crisis spiralling out of control. Its response — seizing land, issuing depreciating leases, and building for everyone — created the highest quality of life metrics in the developed world. At half the tax burden of comparable nations.

Vancouver, by contrast, handed 85% of its land to the market. The result is a city where housing costs more than almost anywhere on earth, and where the average household spends roughly twice what Singaporeans spend to secure shelter.

"Housing is not an investment or a retirement plan — buying and holding real estate means holding something that ultimately goes down in value."
— Elle Griffin, The Elysian

⬡ Land Ownership Model Full public
State owns 90% of all land. All housing and commercial development runs through 30, 60, or 99-year leases — no private freehold. 		Mixed
Mix of private and municipal ownership. Andelsbolig (co-op) sector is significant; some community land trust pilots in social housing. 		Private dominant

Trust holds land in perpetuity on behalf of the community. Residents hold long-term renewable leases — land never re-enters the speculative market.
📉 Housing as Investment? No
Leasehold value declines over time to zero. Housing is a place to live, not a store of wealth. Speculation is structurally minimized. 		Partial
Co-op resale prices capped by government formula. But private market remains fully speculative — Copenhagen is not cheap. 		Aggressively yes
Housing is the city's primary speculative asset class. Average detached home exceeds $1.3M. Generational wealth transfer locked behind private property ownership. 		No — by design
Land appreciation accrues in the trust. Residents build equity in structure and a new "Housing Days" account. A resale formula caps gains and preserves affordability for generations of residents.
🏠 Affordability Mechanism Structural
Government sets rates. CPF mandatory savings fund leases and down payments. Over 80% of the population housed in subsidized HDB flats. 		Moderate
Rent control, ~20% social housing (almene boliger) with broad income eligibility, and co-op resale price limits moderate but don't eliminate cost pressures. 		Inadequate
Weak to no rent control, limited social housing stock (<5%), and no structural mechanism to cap prices. Vacancy tax has been marginal in effect. 		Income-based
Rent set as a percentage of household income, not market rate. Quintile cross-subsidization blends higher-earning residents rents with lower-income neighbours. TFSA/RRSP model of mezzanine financing enables entry.
💰 Land Value Capture Full
State captures all land appreciation via lease auctions and development uplift fees. Developers pay a "development fee" for any increase in land value from permitted changes. 		Partial
Property taxes and betterment levies recover some uplift, particularly in redevelopment zones — but most private land appreciation is still privatized. 		Minimal
Community Amenity Contributions (CACs) are negotiated case-by-case and wildly inconsistent, abandoned and doesn't systemically flow to affordable housing. The vast majority of land value uplift flows to private landowners and developers. 		Full
All land appreciation remains within the trust — permanently. Reinvested into new affordable units, community infrastructure, and the ecological building fund.
🏛️ Revenue & Public Funding Self-funding
Land leases plus Temasek Holdings sovereign wealth fund generate massive non-tax revenue. Tax rate just 13.8% of GDP — less than half the OECD average. 		Tax-funded
High tax base (~46% of GDP) funds robust public services. Some municipal land companies reinvest in housing and transit — but model depends on taxation. 		Insufficient
Property transfer tax and development cost levies are inadequate. City relies heavily on senior government transfers and developer-negotiated contributions - which are simply passed on to buyers, inflating prices. 		Self-sustaining
Trust income from leases compounds over time, funding maintenance, new builds, and community infrastructure without dependence on government transfers or charity.
🗺️ Urban Planning Authority Comprehensive
Urban Redevelopment Authority issues 40–50 year master plans. Knows every lease expiry to the block. Can pull down aging low-density buildings and rebuild for density as population demands — privately owned cities cannot do this. 		Strong
The Finger Plan guides transit-oriented growth around rail corridors. Municipal planning is binding and long-horizon, though not at Singapore's scale of land control. 		Fragmented
Metro Vancouver spans 22 municipalities with conflicting zoning agendas. Effective planning is principally developer-led rather than community-led, favouring maximal extraction over equitable development. 		Participatory
Block-scale planning via Block Share neighbourhood budgeting and planning projects. Community Budget Votes allocate resources by block. Citizen Power Index enables residents to participate in decisions that affect their block, super-block, neighbourhood or city.
🏘️ Social Housing Stock > 80%
The broadest social housing coverage of any developed nation. HDB units are well-built, mixed-income, and located throughout the city — not concentrated in periphery ghettos. 		~20%
Almene boliger are high-quality, geographically distributed, and open to a broad income range — not just the very poor. Still: 80% of residents in the private market. 		< 5%
Chronic undersupply with thousands on waitlists. Social housing is heavily concentrated by poor neighbourhoods and lower income levels — accelerating segregation. 		100% goal
Every unit on PLT land is permanently affordable. Mixed-income model ensures no income-segregated blocks (modeled after False Creek South). Target: all quintiles represented in every block withing Permanent Land Trust.
🔄 Resale & Exit Rules Decaying lease
Land leases depreciate to zero. No land windfall gains possible. When a land lease ends — the cycle of public renewal continues. 		Formula-capped
Co-op resale prices limited by government formula. But the private market — still 80% of housing — faces no cap whatsoever. 		Uncapped
No resale restrictions in the private market. The prevailing norm is maximum extraction on sale. 		Equity-limited
Residents earn limited, formula-based equity in the structure. On sale, that equity is returned to the resident; land value stays in the trust. Affordability is preserved for generations.
🤝 Community Participation Top-down
Highly efficient but paternalistic. The URA plans superbly — but residents have minimal meaningful governance input. What Singapore gains in efficiency, it sacrifices in agency. 		Moderate
Resident boards govern social housing organizations. Participatory budgeting exists in some municipalities. Still largely representative rather than direct democracy. 		Token
Public hearings are required but routinely overridden by developer-driven council decisions and will soon be totally eliminated. Equity participation is weak or non-existent. 		Structural
Participatory Block Budgeting creates pathways for direct democratic engagement. Citizen Power Index demonstrates participation is real and consequential.
🌱 Ecological Stewardship 'City in a Garden'
Greenspace requirements are written into the master plan. Impressive urban canopy. But high-density mandates leave little room for food production or bioregional ecological thinking. 		Leading
Ambitious carbon neutrality targets, mandatory green roofs, world-class cycling infrastructure, and genuine municipal commitment to bioregional ecological planning. 		Aspirational
Strong green building standards on paper. High per-capita ecological footprint in practice. No bioregional governance framework. Ecological metrics are not tied to housing or land policy. 		Foundational
Living Carbon Offsets as micro-asset investments. Food-producing keystone species on every block. Ten Houses / Nine Realms of Cascadia bioregional governance framework. Stewardship quests earn Community Credits — making ecological action economically rational.

What this tells us

Four models, four lessons

🇸🇬

Singapore proves it works at scale

The highest-income, most livable, most affordable developed city in the world runs on a model where 90% of land is never privatized and housing never speculates. The 60 years of evidence is complete. What's missing is the political will to replicate it.

🇩🇰

Copenhagen shows the co-op middle path

Without Singapore's authoritarian powers, Copenhagen achieves significant affordability through co-operative ownership models that cap resale and distribute governance to residents. A 20% partial answer — but still leaves 80% in the speculative market.

🍁

Vancouver is the cautionary tale

Every dimension on this table where Vancouver scores "weak" is a direct result of the same policy choice: allowing 92% of the land to be treated as a private investment vehicle. The city has the highest housing costs, the least social stock, and the most fragmented governance of any of the four models.

🌐

The PLT is Singapore without the state

The Permanent Land Trust achieves Singapore's core structural outcome — land held permanently in common, appreciation captured for the community, speculation structurally impossible — through a community-governed trust rather than state authority. It adds what Singapore lacks: ecological stewardship, participatory governance, and a Community Credits economy that makes belonging economically rational.

The Permanent Land Trust Model

How the Block Share & PLT approach closes the gap

The PLT doesn't require seizing land or electing a Lee Kuan Yew. It requires communities to organize, neighbours to agree, and a legal structure that makes the agreement permanent.

01

Income-Based Rent

Rent is set as a fixed percentage of household income — not a market rate. As incomes rise, the trust earns more. As incomes fall, residents pay less. The building never becomes unaffordable because affordability is defined by the resident, not the market.

02

Quintile Cross-Subsidization

Higher-earning residents rents subsidize lower-earning neighbours within the same building or neighbourhood. Every income quintile (20%) is represented in every PLT building — producing economically diverse communities rather than income-segregated blocks.

03

TFSA / RRSP Mezzanine Financing

Residents and community investors can leverage registered savings (TFSA, RRSP) as mezzanine financing — earning a patient, below-market return while enabling the trust to acquire land without speculative debt. The CPF parallel Singapore used, adapted for Canadian regulatory tools.

04

Community Credits Economy

Block Share's Community Credits and Micro-Asset Systems make ecological stewardship, neighbourhood participation, and mutual aid economically legible. Credits earned through Quests — from planting keystone food trees to attending budget votes — are redeemable for goods, services, and reduced costs within the trust economy.

05

Permanent Land Covenant

A legal covenant — registered on title — ensures the land can never be sold back into the speculative market. Not in 99 years. Not ever. When residents move on, the land stays in common ownership. The affordability of today's unit is guaranteed for generations to come.

06

Bioregional Stewardship

The Ten Houses & Realms of Cascadia framework embeds ecological responsibility into PLT governance. Living Carbon Offsets, food-producing micro-assets, and stewardship quests tie economic participation to the health of the bioregion — something Singapore's density model cannot accommodate.
