01 · The Problem

Every Time Your Block Changes Hands, It Leaves the Neighbourhood.

Commercial property on your street generates value. The café at the corner, the studio above the pharmacy, the ground-floor shop — all of it produces economic activity daily. But when the building that houses these spaces comes to market, the buyer is almost never someone who uses it. They are buying yield. They are buying leverage. And the neighbourhood that built the value of the building gets nothing from the transaction except a rent increase.

This is not incidental. It is the structural logic of how commercial real estate works: ownership follows capital, not community. The people with the most money buy the places where the most people live, and charge the rest for access. The baker who makes the café worth visiting cannot afford the building. The musician who makes the block worth living on cannot afford the studio.

The Blockonomics framework measures this dynamic through two lenses. The Leakage Rate — what proportion of locally generated spending exits to absentee owners — and the Private Tax Burden — the share of working life consumed by payments to remote shareholders rather than to publicly accountable institutions. In most Canadian urban commercial blocks, both numbers are large and growing.

Every time a commercial property sells to a speculative buyer, the neighbourhood loses a piece of the floor it built. The Buy-Back model puts that floor back in community hands.

The conventional answer to this problem is either government intervention — which moves slowly and rarely holds — or co-operative formation — which is complicated, expensive, and requires a cohort with identical resources. The Buy-Back is a third path. It uses existing commercial and legal instruments — the Vendor Take-Back mortgage, the GP/LP limited partnership structure — to make community acquisition possible at scale, across income levels, without waiting for a policy change or a government grant.

02 · The Mechanism

25 Square Feet: The Micro-Asset Model

The insight at the centre of the Buy-Back model is a reframing of scale. A 5,000 square foot commercial property at $850 per square foot — a reasonable Vancouver commercial benchmark — costs $4.25 million. That number puts community ownership out of reach for almost everyone as individuals. But divided into 25 square foot micro-units, each unit costs $21,250. That is a price point accessible to first-time investors across most income ranges, particularly with income-geared deposit requirements.

The micro-unit is not a physical partition of the building. It is a fractional beneficial interest in the underlying property, held collectively through a GP/LP (General Partner / Limited Partner) structure — the same legal vehicle used in every major commercial real estate deal in Canada. What changes is who the limited partners are.

Once the community acquires the property, the LP units represent pro-rata ownership of what the block already uses. As tenants pay rent into the property, distributions flow to LP unit holders. The building appreciates — and when it does, every 25 sq ft stake appreciates with it. The baker cannot afford the building alone. But forty people from the same street, each owning 25 square feet, can.

GP/LP Structure — How It Works

The General Partner (GP)

A community-controlled entity — a local not-for-profit, a land trust, or a purpose-built Block Share GP entity — that holds the property, manages tenants, handles distributions, and acts as the interface between the LP investors and the vendor. The GP is accountable to the LP community, not to outside shareholders.

The Limited Partners (LPs)

Community members who hold micro-units. LP status gives each holder a fractional ownership stake, pro-rata participation in rental income and appreciation, and — for Q4/Q5 investors — the ability to deduct proportionate interest, property taxes, and triple-net expenses against other income. LPs have no management obligations; the GP manages on their behalf.

The Vendor

The selling owner carries the unpaid balance as a Vendor Take-Back (VTB) mortgage at a fixed rate — typically 4.5%. The vendor receives aggregated down payments at closing, then collects scheduled interest payments over 25 years. The vendor exits to community, not to a speculative buyer — and their building continues to serve the neighbourhood that built its value.

03 · Income-Geared Pricing

The Same Asset. Five Different Prices. One Vendor Rate.

The structural challenge of community real estate acquisition is not one of collective will — it is one of collective capacity. A community block is not made up of people with identical incomes. The nurse, the teacher, the student, the tech worker, and the retired landlord all live on the same street. A flat unit price benefits only those who can already afford it.

How It Scales Down
Q1 & Q2 — Subsidised entry

2.75% rate · Scaled deposit · Bi-weekly payments. Lower cash-flow pressure — made possible by the premium carried by higher-income cohorts. Q3 pays the standard rate and anchors the average.

How It Scales Up
Q4 & Q5 — Premium with offsets

Rate above 4.5% · Larger deposit — offset by LP tax deductions on interest, property tax, and triple-net costs. RSP contributions further reduce effective cash outlay at closing.

The cross-subsidy is the engine. The mathematical weighted average across all five quintiles — in an equal-mix cohort — returns exactly what the vendor needs to receive. No external subsidy. No government grant. The community finances itself.

The cross-subsidy is not charity. It is a precision instrument that makes the weighted average vendor rate work — while putting community ownership within reach of every income on the block.

Why Q4 and Q5 Buyers Participate Willingly

Mechanism 01
LP Tax Deductions

As limited partners, Q4/Q5 holders deduct their proportionate share of annual interest, property tax (~1.2% of unit), and triple-net operating expenses (~0.5% of unit) against other income. At a 43% marginal rate this creates a material annual tax saving — partially or fully offsetting the premium rate they carry.

Mechanism 02
RSP Down Payment Strategy

Under arm's-length mortgage rules, RSP funds can flow into qualifying VTB mortgages held by the GP/LP. The RSP contribution generates a tax refund that reduces the effective cash outlay at closing — a pre-tax purchasing mechanism unavailable in conventional real estate.

05 · For the Vendor

Why a Vendor Would Choose Community Over Capital

The Vendor Take-Back structure is not a concession — it is often a superior outcome. A conventional sale returns a lump sum subject to capital gains tax in a single year. A VTB note spreads income over 25 years, smoothing the tax profile and providing predictable, secured returns. The vendor's VTB is registered against the property — they hold a legal claim — and the GP is obligated to make scheduled payments exactly as a bank-financed buyer would be.

Vendor Outcome Comparison

Conventional Sale to Market Buyer

  • Lump sum — full capital gains in one year
  • Buyer's intent: extraction and appreciation
  • Building likely to be repositioned or redeveloped
  • Legacy: sold to whoever had the money
  • No ongoing income stream

Buy-Back — Sale to Community

  • Down payments at close + 25-yr income stream
  • VTB income spread for favourable tax treatment
  • Building continues serving the neighbourhood
  • Legacy: returned the block to the people on it
  • GP manages — vendor is simply the note-holder

There is also a dimension that does not appear on a balance sheet. Vendors who built a business in a neighbourhood have a relationship to that place. Selling to a REIT or an out-of-province investment syndicate ends that relationship cleanly but coldly. Selling to the community means the café stays the café, the studio stays the studio, and the building continues to carry the vendor's name in the neighbourhood's memory — rather than someone else's rebrand.

This is not purely sentimental. Vendors who care about their legacy, their tenants, and the communities they built have concrete, financially sound reasons to choose the Buy-Back — and the model is designed to make that choice easy.

07 · The Commons

One Block at a Time: Reclaiming What Was Already Ours

The Buy-Back is not a standalone transaction model. It is the acquisition arm of a broader framework — the mechanism by which community savings, redirected through the 5% Pledge, are converted into permanent, democratically-held commercial real estate.

Once a building is acquired through the Buy-Back, it does not return to the speculative market. The GP/LP structure connects to the Permanent Land Trust — the legal vehicle that holds land and infrastructure outside the market, permanently. What the community buys back through the Buy-Back, the PLT ensures the community keeps. The Block Fund provides the capital engine. The VTB provides the financing bridge. The PLT provides the legal anchor. Together, they ensure that what the block builds, the block owns.

The Buy-Back is the 3rd Way made concrete. Not a manifesto, not a policy proposal — a mechanism. A vendor, a GP, a community of LP unit holders, a VTB note, and a Permanent Land Trust. Those five components, assembled in the right sequence, move a commercial block from extraction to stewardship permanently and legally. Block by block. 25 square feet at a time.

What the community buys back, the community keeps. The vendor exits to legacy, not to whoever happened to have the most capital at the right moment.
