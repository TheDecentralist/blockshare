Save Our Pond

The wildlife in Vancouver's parks is dying of thirst — and the math says it doesn't have to.

The Argument

For the last several summers, the City of Vancouver has turned off the water supply to park ponds and water features it built itself, citing water restrictions. Vanier Park's pond — created in the 1960s alongside the Museum of Vancouver — is a confirmed example. The fish, birds, frogs, bees, amphibians, and turtles that depend on it have no recourse.

This is not a genuine water shortage problem. It is a governance failure: a bureaucratic decision made without electoral accountability, without exploring the alternatives, and without asking the residents who live beside these spaces and steward them. "Water restrictions" is the excuse on file. It does not hold up against the numbers, and it does not hold up against the alternatives the City has never tried. Nobody loses their seat over a dry pond. Nobody answers to the frogs.

What the City Hasn't Tried

None of this is exotic engineering. These are basic, decades-old practices. The City has tried none of them.

A. Conservation

A modest, voluntary household ask. Never made.

B. Cisterns

Rooftop rainwater capture. Standard in Tokyo, Vancouver's own sister city, since the 1990s. Vancouver has none.

C. Groundwater Wells

Untapped water already reaching English Bay.

D. Drainage Redirection

Ocean-bound stormwater could refill ponds instead.

E. Grey Water Recovery

Reused water for irrigation and pond maintenance.

Vanier Park isn't unique. Vancouver has hundreds of park ponds and water features built by the City and now left to dry every summer — including the pond at Granville Island, alongside its splash park, which is also shut down each season. This campaign starts with the full case on one pond. It is mapping the rest, block by block.

The Bigger Picture: Trees, Snowpack, and What Tokyo Already Knows

The pond isn't the only thing running dry.

In late June 2026, Metro Vancouver imposed its Stage 3 watering restrictions — the most severe in at least a decade — banning all sprinkler and soaker-hose use for trees, shrubs, and flowers. Within days, horticulturalists and parks veterans were raising alarms: Vancouver's urban tree canopy, already shrinking at a documented rate, cannot survive on hand-watering alone. Metro Vancouver's own climate projections warn that the deep spring snowpack in mountainous watersheds is expected to decrease by over 50% compared to present day — the same snowpack that fills the reservoirs that feed the taps that water the parks that keep the ponds full.

These are not separate problems. They are the same problem.

The five alternatives we've been asking the city to try for the Vanier Park pond — cisterns, groundwater wells, drainage redirection, greywater recovery, and conservation infrastructure — are the identical toolkit the urban forestry community has been requesting for years to keep the city's tree canopy alive through increasingly brutal summers. The city has been slow to act on both fronts for the same structural reason: it treats water as a delivered utility, not a locally managed resource.

A Short-Term Signal, Not a Structural Fix

There is one piece of genuinely new information worth noting. Metro Vancouver's Stage 3 restrictions were triggered in part by an infrastructure upgrade: a century-old, metre-wide water pipeline running under Stanley Park was temporarily taken offline for replacement. Officials indicated the new high-pressure main was expected to be back in service by the end of July 2026, at which point Stage 3 restrictions would be reassessed. That's a meaningful near-term reprieve for ponds, boulevards, and struggling street trees.

But a new pipe is not a new water supply. Snowpack acts as water storage — unlike rain, which washes immediately through the system, snow stays in the mountains and releases gradually into spring and summer. Metro Vancouver's South Coast snowpack recorded 61% of normal as recently as February 2026 — a warm winter pattern that forecasters expect to recur. Southern B.C. experienced successive summer streamflow droughts in 2023 and 2024, with flows running 23–43% below historical means. The pipeline fix buys time. It does not buy snowpack.

What Tokyo Already Built

This is not a hard problem. It is an unbegun one.

In the early 1980s, a municipal official in east Tokyo named Makoto Murase designed a water recovery system that collected, filtered, and stored rainwater in large underground holding tanks — easing flooded sewers and providing a resource used for irrigation, toilets, washing, and drinking. After an early version proved successful at Tokyo's Sumo Stadium, the city made underground rainwater tanks mandatory for all new buildings — including the Skytree, Japan's tallest. Today, more than a thousand buildings in Tokyo harvest and recycle their own rainwater.

Tokyo treats water scarcity not as an isolated incident but as a recurring risk to be managed with foresight. Vancouver treats it as a summer inconvenience to be managed with a press release.

The Vanier Park pond is a small ask. Keep the pond full. Try the five alternatives before you drain it. That is still true and still the point of this campaign. But we want to be clear about where this is going: the infrastructure required to protect ponds, water the urban tree canopy, and sustain the city's parks through decades of declining snowpack is the same infrastructure. Cisterns. Wells. Drainage. Greywater. The tools are known. The models exist. The only thing missing is the political will to begin.
