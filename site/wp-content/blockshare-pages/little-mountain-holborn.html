The $334 Million Mirage:
How BC Handed Holborn a Billion-Dollar Site for Free

A forensic analysis of the Little Mountain land deal shows the Province of BC structured a public land sale that — when the financing terms are fully calculated — resulted in taxpayers effectively paying the developer $227 million to own the land, while residents were displaced and social housing was delayed for over 15 years.

In 2008, the BC Liberal government finalised the sale of Little Mountain — a 15-acre social housing site in the heart of Vancouver — to Holborn Holdings, a private development company. The deal was framed as a public-private partnership that would replace 224 aging social housing units with a mixed-income community of 1,400 market and 282 non-market homes.

Seventeen years later, the site sat largely empty. The social housing residents who had called Little Mountain home since the 1950s were dispersed across the city. And embedded in the deal's structure was one of the most consequential financial transfers in Vancouver real estate history — driven not by market forces, but by the terms written into a contract that the Province fought for over a decade to keep secret.

📄 Source: FOI-Released Purchase & Sale Agreement (April 2008)
CBC News obtained the 99-page purchase and sale agreement in August 2021 after protracted Freedom of Information battles. Holborn had filed for judicial review to block its release, then withdrew that application. The confirmed figures from the disclosed contract are: $334M sale price · $35M paid to date · $211M interest-free loan (interest deferred until Dec 31, 2026) · $88M social housing credit.

The Deal Structure: A Government-Financed Land Bank

The confirmed figures from the FOI-released contract show a financing arrangement that bore little resemblance to a standard land sale. Of the $334 million headline price, Holborn paid just $35 million in cash — roughly 10.5% of the stated value.

Of the stated $334M sale price, Holborn paid only $35M in cash. The Province extended $211M as an interest-free loan and $88M as a social housing credit — confirmed by the FOI-released purchase and sale agreement (CBC News, 2021).

Confirmed by FOI Documents

  • $211 million — Interest-free loan from the Province of BC to Holborn; no interest accrues until December 31, 2026
  • $88 million — Credit against the purchase price for delivery of social housing units
  • $35 million — Total cash paid by Holborn across 13+ years after signing
  • No enforceable deadlines — The released contract contains no hard construction milestones that would trigger financial penalties for delay

In effect, the Province became Holborn's private lender — interest-free — on one of the most valuable real estate sites in Western Canada. No commercial bank would offer those terms. No private seller would structure a deal this way. As Vancouver Councillor Christine Boyle put it at the time, this was either an intentional "sweetheart deal" or "gross incompetence."

The Compounding Arithmetic of Delay

Beyond the confirmed financing structure, this analysis models the additional financial benefit Holborn derived from the deal's terms over time. A key note on methodology: the annual 3% price discount shown in the original spreadsheet analysis is a modelled assumption, not a confirmed contract term. The FOI documents confirm the overall structure (the interest-free loan, the $88M credit, no hard deadlines), but the specific rate at which the remaining balance declines per year has not been publicly disclosed from the 99-page agreement. The 3%/year figure should be understood as a working estimate for purposes of this model — the actual rate, if any, remains unclear until the full contract text is more widely reported.

Modelled total financial benefit to Holborn — cumulative price discounts (at estimated 3%/yr) plus imputed interest savings on the $299M government loan at 6% — reaches $561M by 2025. Both components are modelled estimates; the interest-free loan structure is FOI-confirmed.

The Effective Price Went Negative

Perhaps the most arresting visualisation of this deal's structure is what happened to the modelled effective net cost of the land, year by year. When the cumulative discount and imputed interest savings are combined and subtracted from the $334M stated price, the effective cost crosses zero — and keeps falling.

Each bar shows the modelled effective net price Holborn was paying in that year, after cumulative discounts and imputed interest savings on the $299M government loan. Green bars = Holborn still owes the Province. Terracotta bars = the Province was effectively subsidising Holborn's ownership of the land. The crossover occurred in 2018.

The crossover point is significant: by 2018 — just ten years after signing — the financing structure had inverted so completely that the Province was effectively paying Holborn to hold public land. By 2025 that figure reached a modelled negative $227 million. Meanwhile, the 1,400 market units Holborn was entitled to build had grown from a collective estimated value of $427M to over $1 billion.

The Arithmetic of Public Loss (Modelled)

  • Holborn paid $35M cash for 15 acres of prime Vancouver land — confirmed by FOI
  • Province loaned Holborn $299M (interest-free until 2026) — confirmed by FOI
  • Modelled effective net cost by 2025: −$227 million (estimated; 3%/yr discount rate)
  • Market value of 1,400 units by 2025: $1.006 billion at REBGV benchmark prices
  • Net financial swing: over $1.2 billion in developer favour over the delay period
  • Social housing delivered: 53 units completed out of 282 promised; site largely vacant 15+ years

The Market That Kept Climbing

While the site sat idle, Vancouver's housing market continued its relentless ascent. The REBGV benchmark condo price for the Cambie/Vancouver West corridor — directly comparable to what Holborn would eventually build — rose from $305,000 in 2008 to a peak of $805,000 in 2022, before moderating to $718,900 in 2025.

MLS HPI benchmark condo prices in Cambie/Vancouver West rose 135% from the deal date to 2025. Every year of delay transferred additional value appreciation from the public to the developer. Source: REBGV.org.

This is what "land banking with public financing" looks like in practice: the Province absorbed the financial cost of holding 15 acres of some of Vancouver's most valuable land, while the developer sat on $1 billion in unrealised gains.

What Should Have Happened

A genuinely public-interest land agreement might have looked very different:

Alternative Frameworks Worth Considering

  • Community Land Trust — Land retained in public ownership; long-term ground leases to non-profits; appreciation stays with the community
  • Permanent Affordability Covenant — Non-market housing locked in perpetuity, non-dischargeable by future sale
  • Build-First Agreement — Social housing replacement units completed before existing tenants are displaced
  • Market-Rate Financing — Any government loan priced at or near market rates, publicly disclosed at signing
  • Land Value Capture — A portion of appreciation between sale date and development returned to the public
  • Hard Milestones — Enforceable construction deadlines with financial penalties or reversion clauses for delay

None of these are radical — all are in active use in jurisdictions from Vienna to Singapore to Vermont. What they require is political will, and citizens who are informed enough to demand them before deals are signed and buried behind FOI redactions.
