Opening Public Pools
& Open Public Ledgers

A Tale of Two Waterfront Cities — and Two Opposing Values

By Ward Stirrat  ·  The Decentralist
What This Investigation Covers
The Rhetoric vs. The Reality The Nutty Numbers The Private Players The Value Villains The Hopeful Heroes The Open Public Ledger Option

Opening public pools requires an open public democracy — not a closed public bureaucracy.

This is a high-stakes case study for Vancouver as a democracy, and a test of whether the city we live in is a functioning one — or a bureaucracy dressed up as one.

There is a question at the centre of Vancouver’s civic life that no one in official authority has yet been asked to answer publicly, on the record, with numbers. It is not a complicated question. A functioning democracy should be able to answer it in an afternoon.

When public decisions — rezoning approvals, view cone relaxations, density policy changes, upzoning votes — create billions of dollars in new private land value, what share of that value returns to the public? What was created? What was captured? What was actually delivered? And where is the Open Public Value Ledger that shows all three?

That ledger does not appear to exist. Not in any public-facing, consolidated, auditable form. And in its absence, Vancouver’s residents are being asked to accept a city that cannot afford pools — in the same breath as a city that has presided over what may be the largest transfer of publicly-created land value to private hands in its history.

These two facts are not unrelated. They are the same story.

The Framework

Four Questions a Democracy Should Be Able to Answer

The debate about Vancouver’s public infrastructure is almost always framed as a question of scarcity: the city cannot afford pools, community centres, libraries, or childcare because costs are rising and budgets are constrained. That framing accepts as given something that deserves to be examined — the premise that the money was never there to begin with.

A different framing asks four questions instead.

What value was created? When the city approved the Broadway Plan, relaxed 14 view cones, upzoned transit corridors, or enabled the Jericho Lands redevelopment, how much additional development capacity was granted — and what was it worth to the landowners who held it?

What value was targeted? Vancouver’s own Community Amenity Contribution policy established a benchmark: approximately 75 percent of rezoning-generated land value uplift should be captured for public benefit — as pools, community centres, parks, childcare, and affordable housing. What did that policy project the public should receive?

What value was secured? What was actually negotiated — in CACs, Development Cost Levies, affordable housing commitments, and in-kind amenity agreements?

What value was delivered? What was built? Not promised, not planned, not announced in a capital budget ahead of an election. Built. Open. Functioning.

The gap between the answer to question one and the answer to question four is the Public Value Deficit. It is the difference between the city we funded and the city we received. And by any honest order-of-magnitude estimate, that gap in Vancouver is extraordinary.

The numbers involved are not speculative in their broad contours. They emerge from city staff's own planning documents, BC Assessment valuations, and standard land-value modelling applied to announced development capacities.

Rounded to a working figure: approximately $100 billion in new development value, created by public decisions, voted on by public officials, enabled by public infrastructure investment, accruing primarily to private landholders.

Under Vancouver's own 75 percent capture policy, the expected public return on that figure would be approximately $75 billion — in pools, community centres, libraries, parks, childcare spaces, and affordable housing. Whether anything approaching that figure was actually captured, secured, or delivered remains, officially, an open question. No consolidated Open Public Value Ledger exists. No citizen can look up the balance sheet. No auditor has published a comprehensive accounting.

There is a second consequence of mass upzoning that rarely appears in planning presentations: it is permanently and purely inflationary to housing costs. When the city grants blanket tower permissions across entire corridors and neighbourhoods, it does not simply enable taller buildings on a few sites. It immediately inflates the land value of every adjacent parcel that qualifies — permanently, to the ceiling of what the largest and most capitalized developer can afford to bid. The land price is set not by what housing should cost to build, but by what a tower developer can extract from it. Small builders, co-ops, non-profit housing providers, and community land trusts cannot compete against that ceiling. They are mathematically priced out of the market that the city's own upzoning decisions created. Units get smaller. Prices go up. Ground-level amenities disappear into the podium parking math. And every actor in the development ecosystem except the largest, best-financed tower developers is effectively excluded. Vancouver has not accidentally created the conditions for a tower monopoly. It voted for them — and directed the resulting land-value windfall to the very lobby group most capable of capturing it, while telling residents the city cannot afford a pool.

What we know is that Vancouver hasn't opened a new pool since 2009.

This is not a debate about scarcity. It is a debate about stewardship. The question is not whether Vancouver can afford pools. The question is where the money went.

The Case Study

Vancouver House: The Auditor General Puts Numbers on It

The Vancouver House story is worth examining in detail, because it is no longer a matter of estimate or inference. It is formally documented by the City's own Office of the Auditor General, whose 2025 Whistleblower Report — released February 5, 2026, following a complaint by senior development planner Robert Renger — provides a precise, verified illustration of how the public value transfer works at the project scale.

In 2014, the City of Vancouver sold 87 percent of the Vancouver House development site to Westbank for $32 million. City staff had calculated the anticipated land lift from the rezoning at approximately $35 million. On the day of sale, BC Assessment valued the land at $128 million. Westbank immediately obtained $100 million in mortgages against it. The gap between what the land was worth and what the City accepted — nearly $96 million — transferred directly to a private developer through a single transaction.

The Community Amenity Contribution negotiated alongside that rezoning was $10 million against a policy target of $26 million. And the $6 million in-kind component — promised public realm improvements under and around the Granville Bridge, including pedestrian elevators, event infrastructure, lighting, seating, kiosks, and public animation programming — was never properly defined, never enforced, and never delivered. When the auditor general visited the site in October 2025, the public elevator was locked. There were no public washrooms. There was no event infrastructure. There was no public programming. The OAG could find no evidence that any public event had ever taken place in the space. The area is not listed on the City's Plazas and Public Space page.

What is there, under the Granville Bridge, is a spinning chandelier.

The OAG concluded that the city's conduct in this transaction met the legal definition of "waste": the mismanagement of city resources in a negligent manner. City management accepted the finding and promised improved process going forward.

One transaction. One project. One auditor general finding of waste. A $96 million gap in land value, a $16 million shortfall in CAC capture, and $6 million in undelivered amenities. A chandelier.

Now scale that logic across the Broadway Plan, the view cone relaxations, the Vancouver Plan upzoning, and the Jericho Lands. Apply the same pattern of undertargeted CACs, undelivered in-kind commitments, and absent public accountability. The order-of-magnitude implication is not comfortable.

The Comparison

Two Waterfront Cities. One Decided Not to Swim.

The most useful way to understand what Vancouver has chosen not to build is to look at a city of comparable geography, comparable density, and comparable civic ambition that chose differently.

Montreal traces 28 kilometres of waterfront. Vancouver traces 26. Montreal operates approximately 116 public swimming pools for a population of 1.76 million — one pool for every 15,000 residents. Vancouver officially lists 13 — but the Vancouver Aquatic Centre closes permanently on June 28, 2026, dropping the inventory to 12. That is already one pool for every 55,000 residents at 13 facilities, and one pool for every 60,000 once the VAC closes: the lowest per-capita aquatic provision of any major city in Canada, approaching five times worse than a city with which Vancouver shares both its waterfront scale and its aspirations to world-class status.

If Vancouver matched Montreal's service level, residents would have access to roughly 47 pools. Instead, we have 13. Nearly 90 percent of those 13 are at or beyond their expected service life. Ten are in various stages of functional decay. The current deficit against a Montreal standard is 34 pools. Add the closures likely over the coming decade as deferred maintenance compounds, and the projected deficit reaches 44. That is not a planning gap. That is a generational failure of civic stewardship.

Consider what that public value deficit represents in concrete terms.

The Historical Record

A Century of Pools — and a Telling Arithmetic

The standard municipal narrative describes the aquatic system as one of ongoing renewal: old pools replaced by modern ones, capacity maintained or expanded, investment made. That narrative sounds responsible. But it describes a different thing than what residents actually experience — the number of places they can swim.

The distinction matters. A city can tear down five neighbourhood pools and build two larger ones and describe that as progress. But five destinations became two. The residents of the three neighbourhoods that lost pools do not experience progress. They experience absence.

Nine of the eleven currently active pools were built between the 1930s and the late 1970s — before personal computers, before mobile phones, before the internet. The city's aquatic infrastructure was conceived and constructed when a phone was hardwired to the kitchen wall. It has not been meaningfully expanded since. While Vancouver's population has grown from roughly 426,000 in 1971 to more than 720,000 today, the number of places to swim has quietly declined.

This is what the municipal reporting system tends to obscure. The city reports on dollars invested, water surface area upgraded, annual visit counts, and capital expenditure per facility. These are real numbers. But they do not answer the question residents actually experience: are there more places to swim than there used to be? The answer, measured in destinations rather than dollars, is no. There are fewer. And the population served by each one has roughly doubled.

The story being told officially is one of modernization. The story experienced by residents is one of consolidation — fewer destinations, higher density of use, greater distances to travel, reduced neighbourhood access. These are not the same story. The question is which one should inform the city’s aquatic planning going forward.

The Last Pool

A Doubled Population. No Net New Pools Since the 1970s. Only Net Removals.

Vancouver's population has roughly doubled since 1971 — from 426,000 to over 720,000 today. In that same period, the city has built exactly two new pool sites. It has closed five. The net change in aquatic destinations over fifty years of population growth, one Olympic cycle, billions in development revenue, and countless community plans is: negative three. And in seventeen days, it becomes negative four, when the Vancouver Aquatic Centre closes its doors on June 28, 2026.

What makes that silence more galling is what happened during the one moment when federal funding did flow: the 2010 Olympic construction cycle. Percy Norman Pool — a functional, well-used community facility — was demolished to make way for Hillcrest Community Centre and its replacement. The trade looked like progress on paper. In practice, it was a subtraction dressed as an addition.

Hillcrest does have a 50-metre pool — but it was built without the spectator infrastructure, team staging areas, or officials’ facilities that competitive events require. A 50-metre lane is necessary but not sufficient. Compare UBC’s aquatic centre, completed in 2017 for $40 million: 50-metre and 25-metre pools, a dive tank, a leisure pool, a hot pool, and full spectator seating — every component of a complete competitive and community complex. Vancouver took Olympic-era federal investment and produced a facility that cannot host a swim meet, is now overcrowded beyond capacity, and left the city with no functional competition venue when the VAC closes. That was not a budget constraint. That was a set of choices about what kind of city Vancouver intended to become.

⚠  The Vancouver Aquatic Centre closes June 28, 2026.

Vancouver does have a second 50-metre pool at Hillcrest — but Hillcrest cannot host a competitive event. There is no space for competing teams, timing officials, or the parents and spectators that make a meet possible. It is a lap pool, not a competition venue. The VAC has been Vancouver’s only functionally competitive 50-metre facility. When it closes on June 28, that capacity disappears entirely.

A replacement design does exist — but it is the problem, not the solution. The approved proposal replaces a 50-metre competitive facility with a 25-metre lap pool and leisure features at a cost of $171 million and rising: more than four times what UBC spent in 2017 to build a complete competitive complex, and more than eleven times the $15 million estimated for the Marpole outdoor pool that was simply deferred. The city has a design. What it does not have is a design that restores what is being lost.

The city’s official reason for the closure is that the VAC has “reached the end of its functional lifespan.” This framing deserves scrutiny. Kitsilano Pool has been in active use since 1931 — before the Second World War. Second Beach and New Brighton pools date from the same era. Kerrisdale Pool opened in 1955. Several others opened in the 1970s and remain in service today. The city maintains and operates these facilities without declaring them past their lifespan. “End of lifespan” is not an engineering fact applied consistently across the aquatic system. It is a narrative — one that justifies a particular outcome for a particular facility, while the historical record of every other pool in Vancouver tells a different story.

When the VAC closes, Vancouver will have 12 public pools for 720,000 residents — and zero venues capable of hosting a major aquatic event. For that, you will have to leave Vancouver.

The piece of siding that fell off the Vancouver Aquatic Centre did not fall because buildings age. It fell because someone decided, year after year, that maintenance was deferrable. A building does not decay by accident. It decays by neglect, and neglect is a policy choice made by accountable people.

The Pipeline

No Olympians Without Facilities

Canada has historically been a serious force in international aquatic sport. That record was built by communities that invested in 50-metre facilities, diving tanks, timing infrastructure, and the long-term competitive pipelines those facilities make possible. Approximately 95 percent of senior executive leaders across business, government, and civic life played competitive sports. The discipline, team orientation, and physical confidence developed in competitive athletic programs translate across every domain of adult life.

The VAC renewal proposal eliminates the 50-metre pool and replaces competitive capacity with a leisure facility. Hillcrest has 50-metre lanes but no competition infrastructure — no seating, no staging, no officials’ facilities. Without the VAC, Vancouver has no venue where a swimmer can compete in front of their family. Marpole’s pool was deferred. Mount Pleasant’s pool has no timeline. The message accumulating across these decisions is consistent: competitive aquatic sport is not something Vancouver intends to support for its own children. The serious swimmer will eventually face a straightforward choice — remain in a city with nowhere to compete, or move to Montreal, where community infrastructure is treated as a public inheritance.

Vancouver should think carefully about what it means to be the city that tells its athletes: go somewhere else.

The Budget

More Money, Less Pool

The VAC renewal makes the stewardship failure concrete in a single comparison. UBC delivered a complete aquatic complex — 50-metre pool, 25-metre pool, dive tank, leisure pool, hot pool, full spectator seating — for $40 million in 2017. Vancouver is proposing to spend $171 million, and rising, on a 25-metre lap pool, a leisure pool, and recreational features. No 50-metre capacity. No competitive venue. No dive tank. More than four times the cost for a fraction of the function. The city describes this as renewal. It is replacement with something smaller, less capable, and dramatically more expensive per unit of public benefit delivered.

The Pattern

Proposed, Consulted, Deferred, Forgotten

In 2019, Park Board documents outlined a new outdoor aquatic complex for Marpole and Oak Park — lap pool, leisure pool, whirlpool, change facilities — estimated at $15 million as part of a $53.6 million project. By 2022, the pool was deferred. The community centre proceeded. The pool did not.

Mount Pleasant: years of planning, community engagement, public expectation. Deferral. No timeline.

And now: a pool for Sunset, in the capital budget, timed for electoral attention. Residents there have seen this film. The pool appears in a presentation. Politicians reference it at community meetings. The consultation opens. Then the cost estimates arrive, the scope contracts, it becomes a "future phase," and the future phase becomes a footnote. The community centre proceeds. The pool does not. The chandelier, presumably, is on order.

The pattern is not random. Amenities that serve concentrated private interests are funded, approved, and built. Amenities that serve dispersed public needs are planned, consulted, deferred, and quietly removed from scope. The pool is always the variable. The tower lobby is never the variable.

The city does not announce that it has decided to be a worse place to live. It simply defers the pool. And then defers it again. And a piece of siding falls off.

The Demand

The Missing Ledger — and Why Its Absence Is a Democratic Problem

In a functioning democracy, the relationship between public decisions and public benefit should be traceable. When government creates private value — through zoning, through infrastructure investment, through planning approvals — the public should be able to verify what was created, what was captured, and what was delivered. Not as a technical exercise for specialists. As a basic condition of civic trust.

What does not currently appear to exist in Vancouver is a consolidated, public-facing balance sheet that answers those three questions across all of the city's major planning decisions of the past decade. No citizen can pull up a document that shows: here is the land lift we created through the Broadway Plan; here is what our policy said we should capture; here is what we actually negotiated; here is what was built. The Open Public Value Ledger is missing. And its absence is not incidental — it is the condition under which the gap between public value created and public value delivered can persist without triggering accountability.

The auditor general's Vancouver House finding demonstrated what happens when one transaction is subjected to scrutiny. A $128 million asset sold for $32 million. A $26 million CAC target that became $10 million. A $6 million in-kind commitment that became a chandelier. The OAG called it waste. Now apply that scrutiny to the full Open Public Value Ledger — the Broadway Plan, the view cone changes, the Vancouver Plan, Jericho Lands — and ask what a comprehensive public accounting would show.

That is the question Vancouver's residents should be demanding their elected officials answer. Not whether the city can afford pools. Whether the city can account for where the money went.

The Legacy

The Avoidance of Legacy

Legacy infrastructure is what a city builds when it believes in its own future. Facilities that will serve the next generation. Pipelines that will train tomorrow's athletes. Public spaces that outlast the council that approved them. Montreal builds legacy. It chose to.

Vancouver has built something else.

The avoidance of aquatic infrastructure.

The avoidance of competitive sport pipelines.

The avoidance of public value capture.

The avoidance of maintained public assets.

The avoidance of transparent accounting.

The avoidance of a public ledger.

The avoidance of responsible stewardship.

This is Vancouver's legacy — the one we are choosing not to build.

Pools serve working families who cannot afford private fitness memberships. They serve children learning to swim — a literal life-safety skill. They serve competitive athletes who need 50-metre water and dive tanks and timing infrastructure. They serve elders for whom aquatic exercise is therapeutic, not recreational. They serve a city in a heat emergency. In a city with 26 kilometres of waterfront and a climate that is warming, public water access is the city's character made visible.

Vancouver's residents deserve an Open Public Value Ledger. Not of what was planned. Of what was created, what was captured, and what was built. The chandelier is beautiful. It spins. It does not teach a child to swim, train an Olympian, or cool a city in a heat dome. And it does not constitute a public return on $100 billion of publicly-created wealth.

If Vancouver matched Montreal's service level, there would be 47 public pools in this city instead of 13 — 34 more places for a child to learn to swim, for a senior to move without pain, for a serious athlete to train for a national championship, for a resident to cool down in a summer that will only get hotter.

Instead: 10 barely functioning pools decay without a maintenance timeline. 34 pools are missing with no construction date. The Sunset pool joins the queue behind Marpole and Mount Pleasant. The VAC renewal spends $171 million to eliminate the 50-metre pool and the dive tank. A $128 million piece of public land was sold for $32 million. The CAC policy target of $26 million became $10 million. The $6 million in promised public realm amenities was never built. The auditor general documented it as waste. And $100 billion in publicly-created land value changed hands with no Open Public Value Ledger to account for where it went.

The chandelier spins. Vancouver hasn't opened a new pool since 2009.

Open the Public Value Ledger — and then open the 47 pools Vancouver is missing to match Montréal’s world-class public aquatic infrastructure.
