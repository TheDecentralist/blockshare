Claims Parser — Broadway Plan
Official talking points parsed against available data and documented evidence. Click any claim to expand.
Talking Point "The Broadway subway is the only solution capable of handling future ridership demand." ▼
Demand forecasts for the Broadway corridor have been consistently revised. Surface BRT systems in comparable corridors (Bogotá TransMilenio, Ottawa Transitway) have exceeded 40,000 passengers/hour — comparable to SkyTrain capacity — at a fraction of the capital cost. The "capacity ceiling" argument assumes no operational optimization and ignores that surface systems can be upgraded incrementally as demand materializes, rather than requiring full capital commitment upfront.
⬡ Source: ITDP BRT Standard 2016 · TransMilenio capacity data · [PLACEHOLDER — study citations pending]
Misleading "The subway serves the Broadway corridor from VCC-Clark to UBC." ▼
The approved subway terminates at Arbutus — not UBC. UBC is the corridor's largest single destination, generating an estimated 50,000+ daily trips. The "Broadway Plan" framing consistently implies UBC connectivity that does not exist in the approved scope. The gap between Arbutus and UBC (approximately 5km) has no funded solution, meaning the largest demand generator remains unserved after $2.83B of investment.
⬡ Approved terminus: Arbutus Street · UBC daily trips: [PLACEHOLDER] · Arbutus–UBC gap: ~5km unserved
Partial "Tunnelling is necessary because Broadway cannot accommodate surface transit." ▼
Broadway's right-of-way is 30m wide — sufficient for a dedicated surface transit corridor. The claim that surface operations are incompatible with Broadway traffic conflates current configuration (without transit priority) with what a redesigned street could accommodate. Comparable arterials in Zurich (Bahnhofstrasse), Amsterdam, and Melbourne run surface trams at high frequency without the grade separation argument holding. The tunnelling preference reflects political and developer interests in TOD density premiums around underground stations, not engineering necessity.
⬡ Broadway ROW: 30m · Comparable arterials: [PLACEHOLDER] · TOD density uplift around subway stations: [PLACEHOLDER]
Talking Point "This is a once-in-a-generation investment in sustainable transportation." ▼
The "once-in-a-generation" framing is precisely the problem. Locking $2.83B into a fixed 6km tunnel forecloses the adaptive flexibility that transit networks need as urban mobility shifts. A surface alternative deployed at 20% of the cost leaves 80% of the capital envelope available for network expansion, cycling infrastructure, or technology transition. The generational argument is used to suppress scrutiny of alternatives — the opposite of what a genuine long-horizon investment requires.

Claims Parser — Vancouver Pools
Official framing parsed against lifecycle accounting and peer city data.
Talking Point "The Killarney and Hillcrest pools represent a major investment in aquatic infrastructure." ▼
Signature facility investment has been used to mask the aggregate deficit. Two well-funded flagship facilities do not offset the deferred maintenance backlog across the remaining stock. When measured against per-capita access — the metric that actually determines whether residents can use public aquatic infrastructure — Vancouver remains the worst-served major city in Canada. The Parks Board communication consistently highlights new builds while avoiding lifecycle accounting of the broader portfolio.
⬡ Vancouver pools per 100k: ~0.8 · Canadian major city avg: ~2.0+ [PLACEHOLDER]
Misleading "Pool closures are temporary — facilities will reopen after renovation." ▼
Several Vancouver pool closures framed as "temporary" have become permanent or indefinitely deferred. The renovation funding pathway is routinely cited without committed capital plan line items. When deferred maintenance reaches critical failure, the fiscal case for renovation collapses and closure becomes permanent by default. This is the classic Defer→Critical Failure pathway — not a renovation strategy.
⬡ Facilities closed "temporarily" vs. subsequently reopened: [PLACEHOLDER — Parks Board capital plan audit needed]
Partial "Pool construction and operation is prohibitively expensive." ▼
Construction cost is real — but the framing obscures two things. First, deferred maintenance is not cheaper than maintenance: the compounding cost of neglect consistently exceeds proactive investment by 3–5× at end of lifecycle. Second, distributed smaller facilities (25m community pools) cost substantially less than signature 50m competition facilities and serve more residents per dollar. The "too expensive" argument is applied selectively to justify inaction, not to guide efficient investment.
